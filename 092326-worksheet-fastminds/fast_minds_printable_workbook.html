Thinking Errors to Watch For

Self-critical thoughts often mismatch the facts. Check off any patterns you recognize in your “hottest” thought above.

All-or-nothing thinking — seeing a situation in only two extremes.
Overgeneralizing — treating one setback as an endless pattern.
Discounting the positive — waving off what actually went well.
Jumping to conclusions — assuming you know how it will turn out, or what someone else thinks.
Magnifying or minimizing — blowing a problem up, or shrinking a strength down.
Catastrophizing — expecting the worst possible outcome.
Emotional reasoning — treating a feeling as if it were a fact.
Personalizing — taking the blame for something that wasn't fully yours to own.
Draining Out the Emotion

1. Notice the pattern. Catch yourself blaming or catastrophizing before it snowballs.

2. Have a go-to phrase ready. A short, even lighthearted line can defuse the moment and put you back in a problem-solving frame of mind.

Reward Yourself Often

A FAST MIND sustains effort better with frequent, small rewards than with one big payoff far in the future. Daily rewards don't need to be expensive — a favorite playlist, a bubble bath, time with a pet. For the tasks you dread most, allow yourself a bigger reward, or consider outsourcing the task entirely. It helps to brainstorm a whole list of rewards at once, so you're not stuck inventing one in the moment — and to refresh the list once the old ones stop feeling special.

Steps to Practicing Habits

Most habits form through repeated practice, backed by two allies: a peripheral brain (something outside yourself that carries the reminder) and accountability (someone or something that notices whether you followed through).
Start smaller than feels necessary.
Attach the new habit to a routine you already do.
Track it somewhere visible for at least two weeks.
Expect setbacks and restart without judgment.
Adjust the habit's size or trigger if it keeps failing.
Celebrate the streak, not just the end goal.

Practicing Productive Communication

Productive communication states what you need clearly instead of hoping someone infers it. Practice a specific "ask" below.
Before a hard conversation
▪Imagine explaining it as calmly as you would to a neutral interviewer.
▪Aim to listen more than you talk.
▪Before you respond, check: is this actually a reply to what they just said?
▪Jot down the key points you want to land, ahead of time.
▪Match their level of emotional intensity rather than escalating past it.
▪Agree on what the conversation is actually about before you dive in.

A quick recap to keep this chapter oriented before you move on.

Key Points
